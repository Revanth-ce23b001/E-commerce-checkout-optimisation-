# Phase 4 — Stage 2: M2 (post-selection), under the A47 ruling

Generated by `scripts/07_fit_m2.py`. Every figure is measured on the dataset or
read from `data/truth/_truth.json`. Nothing is quoted from spec prose.

This report exists because the §8.4 escalation raised in
`reports/phase4_m1.md` §6 was **ruled on** — option 3, per-tier thresholds, with
three conditions. `docs/phase4_escalation.md` is the standalone record of the
finding and the ruling. This is the model fitted under it.

M2 is the **post-selection** model: it sees `payment_method`, `paid_via_switch`
and `payment_attempt_count`, which M1 had to project away. That is the only
difference in the feature set, and it is why **p\* = 0.2576 applies here and
did not apply to M1** — p\* is the break-even of a *COD* order, and M2 is the
first model that knows whether the order is one.

### What is not in the model, and why

## 5. The p\*-anchored tiering

M2 is the first model entitled to §8.3's economic cut-lines, and they are cut
lines, not percentiles: LOW below 10%, HIGH at or above p\* = 0.2576, where a
COD order's expected contribution margin is exactly zero.

| tier | definition | orders | share_pct | expected_share_pct | observed_rto |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LOW | p < 0.10 | 9002 | 39.9700 | 45.0000 | 0.0534 |
| MED | 0.10 <= p < 0.2576 | 7200 | 31.9700 | 38.0000 | 0.1761 |
| HIGH | p >= 0.2576 (p*) | 6318 | 28.0600 | 17.0000 | 0.3890 |

**The HIGH tier is 28.1% of traffic, not the ~17% §8.3 expected.**
That is not a modelling error and it should not be tuned away. §8.3's shares were
priors written against the project's headline blended RTO rate of 16.53%; this
population is the *uncensored window*, whose base rate is 0.1868. A higher
base rate puts more mass above a fixed economic threshold. The threshold is the
thing that was pre-committed to; the share is an output.

## 6. The ruling applied — per-tier thresholds

### 6.1 Which levers are rationed by geography (condition 1)

Restrictive interventions rank within tier. Offers use the global score. The
ruling's phrasing is the test: **carrots are not rationed by geography; sticks
are.**

| id | intervention | kind | threshold basis | why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Prepaid incentive | offer | global | a discount. Rationing it by geography would withhold money from Metro customers who qualify on risk, which is not what §8.4 protects against. |
| B | COD fee | RESTRICTIVE | per-tier | prices an option the customer already had. The archetypal stick. |
| C | Trust-building checkout | offer | global | messaging. Costs the customer nothing and removes nothing. |
| D | Partial payment | RESTRICTIVE | per-tier | conditions COD on an upfront payment. A restriction with a softer edge, not a different kind of thing. |
| E | Smart payment recommendation | RESTRICTIVE | per-tier | reorders and DE-EMPHASISES. Ruled restrictive: de-emphasis is a withdrawal of salience, and salience is what a default is made of. Carries the one-tap constraint below. |
| F | Payment-reliability routing | offer | global | fixes a broken rail. §10.3 calls it the only intervention with no downside on any axis. |
| G | COD gating (COD withdrawn) | RESTRICTIVE | per-tier | the hardest form of the restriction. Not a lettered §10.1 lever; named here because the ruling names it. |

**`E` was reclassified as restrictive (decision A48).** It was first listed as an
offer because reordering removes nothing. That was wrong: E **de-emphasises** as
well as emphasises, and for a payment method chosen by 62% of orders largely out
of habit, salience *is* the option. A lever expected to move prepaid share by
3-6pp through position alone exercises the same power as a fee while requiring
none of a fee's disclosure. It ranks per-tier, and it carries a hard floor into
the Phase 5 PRD:

> COD must remain reachable in ONE TAP in every variant of E. Reordering and de-emphasis are permitted; an extra tap, a hidden menu, a collapsed accordion or a confirmation interstitial is not. §10.1 already draws this line — 'emphasis is acceptable; hiding or burying COD is not' — and E is the only lever that can cross it without any copy changing.

different shares in different tiers, because clean records are not evenly

vetoed and Metro's final rate lands lowest. **The residual spread is caused by a
protection, not by the score.** Measured rather than asserted:

### 6.3 What the constraint costs (condition 2)

Both arms restrict the **same selected volume** — the `selected` column — so the
only difference between them is *which* orders they choose. The `_n` columns are
what survives the overlay veto and differ slightly for the reason in §6.2.

The counterfactual is stated once and applied identically to both arms: a
restricted order **does not happen**, so its realised contribution margin goes to
zero and the policy's effect on it is `-cm`. An RTO carries a negative margin, so
removing it is a gain; a delivered order carries a positive one, so removing it is
a loss. This is §10.2's own frame — *above p\*, an abandoned order is a saving* —
and it puts no behavioural parameter anywhere in the arithmetic.

**The price of the fairness constraint, at the §8.3 volume of 17%, is
₹172,634 across a 22,520-order test window — ₹54.94 per
restricted order, or ₹3.92 Cr a year** at the derived annualisation
factor of 227.3. That is the number the ruling asked to have on the record.

For scale: the project's headline RTO exposure is ~₹165 Cr a year. The fairness
constraint costs **2.4% of that**, and it buys a policy that can be
defended to a customer, a regulator, or a journalist.

**The price is robust to the behavioural response.** If restricted COD orders
switch to prepaid rather than abandoning — earning the prepaid economics measured
on this dataset, ₹96.09 per switched order — both arms improve together
and the gap barely moves:

The switch shares are a **sweep, not an estimate**. Nobody has measured the switch
rate on this platform; §10.1's -20 to -30pp figures are Phase 1 priors written
down to be tested. What the sweep establishes is that the answer to "what does
fairness cost" does not depend on picking a point inside that range.

Three things, in the order that matters:

1. **Tier-3's absolute restriction rate falls from 42.3% to 15.0%.**
   Under the global rule the policy restricted more than **two in five** Tier-3
   orders. Under the ruling it restricts about one in seven. The answer to
   condition 3 is that the absolute exposure does not merely become *equal*, it
   becomes roughly a third of what it was.

2. **The cost lands exactly where the objection said it would.** Metro's
   restricted set has a mean score of 0.1551 and a realised RTO rate of
   15.8% — and **96.8% of those orders score below p\***,
   which means their expected contribution margin as COD orders is *positive*. The
   policy restricts them anyway. That is not a defect in the implementation; it is
   the trade, and §6.3 is what it costs.

3. **Under-restriction is the mirror image, and it is the part a fairness
   framing can hide.** Tier-3's restricted orders carry a 56.0% realised
   RTO rate against Metro's 15.8%. Per-tier thresholding does not make
   Tier-3 safe; it stops the policy from *pricing* how unsafe it is. Both tiers
   are cut at their own 83rd percentile, so a Tier-3 order just below that cut
   goes unrestricted while a Metro order just above it is restricted at
   **3.6x less risk**. Nothing about equalising the ratio makes
   that comparison go away, and anyone presenting this policy should say so
   before someone else does.

## 7. Verdict

M2 test AUC **0.7684** clears the §9.4 gate of 0.72 and beats the full three-rule §9.3 floor (0.6806) by 8.8pp. Calibration, which outranks AUC here because the thresholds are absolute probabilities, holds at a slope of 0.9466. Full risk-based pricing is permitted on discrimination.

**The challenger does not ship.** -0.34pp against a required +3.00pp. The scorecard stays primary, which is what blueprint §9.3 pre-committed to before either number existed.

**FA-01 passes under the ruled per-tier thresholds — 1.44x against a 2.5x limit, at every volume tested.** The same model under global thresholds fails at every volume, which is the M1 escalation reproduced: the ruling changed the policy, not the score.

**The constraint costs ₹172,634 on the test window — ₹54.94 per restricted order, ₹3.92 Cr a year.** That is roughly 2.4% of the project's ~₹165 Cr headline exposure, and it is the price of a policy that can be defended outside the building. The number is on the record as the ruling required, and it is robust to the behavioural response: under a 0-50% COD-to-prepaid switch sweep it moves by under 4%.

**What per-tier thresholding does NOT fix.** Tier-3's absolute restriction rate falls from 42.3% to 15.0% at the §8.3 volume, which answers condition 3 in the direction it was asked. But 97% of the Metro orders the policy now restricts score BELOW p*, so their expected contribution margin is positive and restricting them is a deliberate loss — while Tier-3 orders go unrestricted at 3.6x the realised RTO rate of the Metro orders that are restricted. Equalising the ratio moved the unfairness; it did not delete it. §6.4 states both halves.
